2 General Principles of
Classiﬁcation (a reprise)

the

APG

The authors of the previous APG classiﬁcations have
recognized that multiple classiﬁcations may be derived
from published evidence, and the choice of which to accept
has always depended on the author′s taxonomic philosophy
and approach. There is more than one way to cut up a
phylogenetic tree, and authors have varied opinions on how
best to do this to produce a classiﬁcation. In APG (1998), the
authors explained the process used to determine the number
of orders to recognize and the criteria that were considered
important. APG (1998) previously recognized the families and
orders that had been in the prevailing morphologically based
classiﬁcations of that time to provide stability and familiarity,
but these did not always have the traditional composition.
This task was made easier because in the 1970s, substantial
agreement had developed among the systems being
proposed. However, the authors of APG (1998) made a
conscious eﬀort to minimize the number of orders to keep
the system as simple as possible. Thus, the general
arrangements of the monocot orders and families were
those proposed by Dahlgren et al. (1985). The original APG
(1998) for all angiosperms was similar in structure to that
proposed by Dahlgren (1975), which was notable in the
use of morphological cladistics to provide a picture of
angiosperm relationships. Dahlgren (1975) recognized
roughly 30 superorders, which is similar in number and
names to the 40 orders in APG (1998). This general structure
has been maintained in all subsequent APG iterations, which
have merged traditional concepts with the results of modern
DNA analyses informed by the input of experts. To maintain
stability and mediate diverse opinions when changes have
been proposed, a set of general guiding principles was
established, which we reiterate below.
2.1 Monophyly
Monophyly has been the most important principle since the
ﬁrst version of APG (1998; published without a number).
Before this, paraphyly and polyphyly in angiosperm classiﬁcations based on morphology were addressed by Judd et al.
(1994), who proposed a series of revised family limits for

cases of non‐monophyly, which were incorporated into APG
(1998). Other cases of paraphyly, that is, Amaranthaceae/
Chenopodiaceae, Boraginaceae/Hydrophyllaceae, and Sapindaceae/Aceraceae/Hippocastanaceae, were resolved in APG
(1998). Since then, circumscriptions of several other
problematic families have been substantially revised to
accommodate new results of molecular phylogenetic studies,
for example, Acanthaceae, Aristolochiaceae, Lamiaceae,
Orobanchaceae, Scrophulariaceae, and Verbenaceae (APG
IV, 2016). At the ordinal level, there have been fewer
problems with non‐monophyly; however, given that the APG
orders were mostly based on DNA results and unique to APG,
such problems should not have been expected.
Monophyly as a guiding principle becomes more complicated when faced with evidence of species/clades created by
hybridization, generating numerous instances of gene‐tree
discordance that led to a lack of clear evidence of
relationships (Jeﬀroy et al., 2006; Steenwyk et al., 2023).
Speciation events are real and have been empirically
demonstrated (reviewed in Hernández‐Hernández et al., 2021).
However, some genomes have been shown to be “porous,”
with a few loci subject to extensive gene ﬂow (Lexer
et al., 2009; Campbell et al., 2018), whereas most genes in
these same species do not show gene ﬂow. We must,
therefore, expect to encounter discordant gene trees in some
groups despite no other evidence of hybridization. Nonetheless, some tests may help to determine which gene trees
have signs of incomplete lineage sorting (also referred to as
hemiplasy, deep coalescence, retention of ancestral polymorphisms, or trans‐species polymorphisms) or introgression
(e.g., resulting from horizontal gene transfer or hybridization;
Smith et al., 2020; Morales‐Briones et al., 2021; Singhal
et al., 2021; Komarova & Lavrenchenko, 2022), the former
being unreliable for phylogeny reconstruction and the latter
being directly responsible for gene‐tree discordance.
Addressing reticulation in classiﬁcation is problematic at any
level, but despite evidence of hybridization in the ancestry of
some older clades (recognized as higher taxa, such as orders
and families), these clades are generally monophyletic, even
though we expect that in some gene trees, they may be
otherwise. Taxa with a hybrid origin can be named on this basis
(e.g., Knapp et al., 2004, for allotetraploids in Nicotiana,
Solanaceae). We do not propose to assign taxa of hybrid origin
to the same taxon as one of their parents, even if the hybrid
genome has retained more genes from that parent. Instead, we
recommend naming them in the same manner as any family or
order, but note their putative hybrid origin (e.g., as we have for
Ericales in this classiﬁcation, which appears to be hybrid in
origin; Stull et al., 2020). However, understanding more general
patterns of diversiﬁcation when both parental and hybrid clades
are included is less straightforward, but purely for the purposes
of a classiﬁcation, understanding the causes for incongruence is
not strictly essential. Other naming strategies are possible (e.g.,
use of the PhyloCode approach; de Queiroz et al., 2020), but
these are beyond the scope of the APG classiﬁcation.

demise of the COM clade), but at present, we cannot
ascertain their parentage or other potential processes

2.2 Stability
The APG stability principle states that if a currently accepted
APG family or order has been shown to be monophyletic, it
will not be subsequently split. Although not an issue in the
ﬁrst two iterations of APG (1998, 2003), stability soon became
nearly as important as monophyly. Incorporation of DNA
sequence data into classiﬁcation has in some cases, changed
names and families of commercially important plants. There
are few scientiﬁc ﬁelds in which advances in understanding
fundamental subjects, in this case understanding phylogenetic
relationships, have had such unlucky shortcomings and real
economic costs (Valleau, 2004; Mabberley, 2017).
Achieving consistency in the application of names requires
that APG family and order names should not be altered
unless (i) new results demonstrate non‐monophyly or (ii)
small families (often monogeneric/monotypic) are better
treated as members of their sister families (e.g., expansion of
Orobanchaceae to include several recently proposed small
families with only brief histories of recognition/use).
However, any such changes are likely to result in altered
distributions of apomorphies for families/orders, and therefore, it is essential to balance these often‐conﬂicting issues
when contemplating changes.
The users of the APG classiﬁcation are varied and cover
numerous scientiﬁc ﬁelds beyond phylogenetics, systematics,
and taxonomy, including agriculture, anatomy, biochemistry,
conservation, developmental biology, ecology, ﬂoristics,
horticulture, linguistics, paleobotany, pharmacognosy, physiology, and plant/animal/microbial interactions. Stability aids
in literature searches by non‐systematist users who are
unfamiliar with the history of plant classiﬁcation. Naturally,
the APG system of classiﬁcation is of great relevance to
taxonomists and systematists, but changes in a classiﬁcation
aﬀect numerous stakeholders across many other ﬁelds, for
whom the ﬁne details of phylogenetic relationships may be
less important. It is nonetheless vital to carefully assess the
broader impacts of any proposed changes (Mabberley, 2017).
APG has a long history of using the advice of experts and
results of phylogenetic studies produced by the phylogenetics community, particularly in the ﬁrst two versions
(1998, 2003), and this community‐based content has been
retained and predominates in the subsequent updates.
However, it has not always been possible to reconcile
some classiﬁcations proposed by experts with the principles
that govern the APG classiﬁcation. There are solutions to this
dilemma (see below) that permit stability to be maintained
while providing a more thorough and perhaps more
synapomorphy‐rich classiﬁcation that many users will ﬁnd
valuable.
In the various iterations of APG, the number of families
recognized has generally decreased over time as relationships have been clariﬁed: 462 families in APG (1998) versus
416 families in APG IV (2016). APG V accepts 407 families.
Reduction was especially obvious when the bracketed

system in APG II (2003), which allowed users to choose

APG III (2009). This dual system was instituted so that
experts could use narrower limits if they wished and still
follow APG, while permitting other more general, nonsystematist users to recognize more broadly deﬁned families.
The elimination of choice in APG III (2009) was due to many
non‐systematist users saying that they could not make these
decisions based on their limited knowledge and wanting the
APG compilers to abandon this option. In nearly all cases, the
broader circumscriptions replaced the narrower ones, again
with feedback from many users, and subsequently, the
number of families recognized decreased: 457 families in APG
II (2003) to 413 families in APG III (2009). Recognition of
monofamilial orders was accepted since APG IV (2016), and
these have been maintained in APG V. This adjustment in APG
philosophy (since APG II, 2003) has been fundamental to
maintaining stability of orders. In contrast to the number of
families, the number of orders has increased, from 60 orders
in APG II (2003) to 66 in APG V, largely due to an increase in
those that are monofamilial.
With APG III (2009), stability became paramount, and APG
IV (2016) and APG V (this paper) have involved only carefully
assessed alterations of APG III, informed by the extensive
nuclear and largely congruent plastid DNA evidence that
provides the framework of APG V. In an assessment of the
eﬀects of APG on treatments of the ﬂora of New Caledonia,
Gotty & Pillon (2025) found that since 2012 (when the APG
III, 2009, classiﬁcation was implemented), family‐level
changes have been reduced to zero. This stability led these
authors to conclude that “this classiﬁcation can now be
safely applied outside the world of scientiﬁc publication.”

3 Operational Guidance and Clade‐speciﬁc
Issues
An alternative approach for specialists who believe that the
broader family limits in APG are unsatisfactory is that taken
for Asparagales, in which APG III used broad limits for
Amaryllidaceae, Asparagaceae, and Xanthorrhoeaceae (now
Asphodelaceae due to subsequent conservation; Klopper
et al., 2018). For those interested in the ﬁner details of
relationships in these clades, Chase et al. (2009) published a
subfamily classiﬁcation of Amaryllidaceae, Asparagaceae, and
Asphodelaceae (as Xanthorrhoeaceae). Chase et al. (2009)
admitted that there were no morphological synapomorphies
for some of the more broadly delimited families, but equally,
there are no readily apparent non‐DNA synapomorphies for
many of the subfamilies of Chase et al. (2009). Thus, neither
approach resulted in easily identiﬁable families/subfamilies. If
family circumscriptions are going to be diﬃcult to explain,
then fewer, more broadly deﬁned families may be easier for
non‐specialist users to remember and use than many, more
diﬃcult to explain families. Of course, many well‐established
families have had to be morphologically recircumscribed

families (e.g., Acanthaceae, Gesneriaceae, Lamiaceae, and
Scrophulariaceae). This resulted in families that are diﬃcult
