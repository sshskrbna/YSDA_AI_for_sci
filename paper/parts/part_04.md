3.7 The parasite/holomycotroph problem
Parasites and holomycotrophs generally have been unsatisfactorily placed in the past based on organellar DNA analyses
because the typical genes used are absent or highly modiﬁed in
these groups. In the nuclear DNA sequencing results of Zuntini
et al. (2024), these taxa were often frustratingly diﬃcult to
incorporate into matrices due to the low recovery of highly
divergent genes. It can reasonably be expected that ﬂowering
plants with modiﬁed plastomes (i.e., parasites and holomycotrophs) will see reduced recovery of nuclear genes associated
with organellar function, as is the case for many Angiosperms353
target genes (Johnson et al., 2019). The same applies to highly
reduced aquatics, such as Ceratophyllaceae, Hydrostachyaceae,
and Podostemaceae. Even with reasonable recovery, the highly
divergent genes sequenced may possibly disrupt evidence of
relationships with other families in these orders. We propose
treating placements of several of these families as equivocal and
position them only tentatively in orders, in some cases removing
them from families in which they were formerly placed based
solely on plastid and/or mitochondrial studies. In another case
(Dioscoreales), we note the lack of resolution and support and
propose to retain the APG IV classiﬁcation, which includes
families likely to prove non‐monophyletic (see below).

5 Discussion
Previous APG classiﬁcations, which were based almost solely
on plastid DNA analyses, have been largely conﬁrmed by
recent studies with large‐scale taxonomically informed
sampling of hundreds of nuclear genes. As with other
iterations of APG, we intend APG V to serve as a framework
for communication not only among specialists but also more
general users across diverse areas of biology in the broadest
sense and other ﬁelds. Specialists may wish to propose
alternative classiﬁcations for families and orders; we do not
intend that APG should impede such proposals and
discussions. Indeed, other classiﬁcations should be published
and used as needed. However, we advocate that these
should refer to the APG V classiﬁcation so that interested
general readers can follow the discussions and controversies.
As stated above, the current composition of families and
orders in APG V will remain unchanged unless an APG family
or order is shown not to be monophyletic, with the caveat
noted above for condensation of small families with their
close relatives. However, we note that the relative arrangement of orders, especially in the core eudicots, is susceptible
to change. Familial relationships in orders are also likely to
change as new evidence emerges, and these are listed here
(Appendix A) in an alphabetical sequence. In a few cases, we
note below some remaining issues that may require
modiﬁcation of current circumscriptions when clariﬁed.

6 Notes on Problematic Groups and
Changes
1. Dioscoreaceae are a prime example of phylogenetic
analysis problems caused by holomycotrophs, which
mostly have reduced and altered plastid and mitochondrial genes/genomes (Shepeleva et al., 2020; Garrett
et al., 2023). Gene recovery with target sequence
capture has been lower than that for photosynthetic
genera (Zuntini et al., 2024), most likely due to the
divergent nature of these genes. Additionally, the
nuclear genes that have been sequenced diverge
considerably from those in the photosynthetic taxa
(Soto Gomez et al., 2025). This introduces two problems:
(i) uncertain placement and (ii) disruption of the
relationships among the photosynthetic taxa because
of putative long‐branch attraction problems and uneven
sampling of genes in the data sets. The species of
Afrothismia Schltr. are especially problematic regarding
(i) and (ii) above (Soto Gomez et al., 2025). Formerly
treated as a member of Thismiaceae, Afrothismia was
recently proposed as a family, Afrothismiaceae, by Cheek
et al. (2024), who focused their work on the
mitochondrial study of Lin et al. (2022). Based on the
topology of that study, monogeneric Dioscoreaceae,
Trichopodaceae, and Taccaceae were also recognized
(Stenomeris Planch. was not included there). Cheek et al.
(2024) listed the morphological diﬀerences between the
dioscoreoid genera and argued that they are too
diﬀerent to be included in a single family. These views
reﬂect an interpretation of what are “signiﬁcant

2. Ixiolirion Fisch. ex Herb. is supported as sister to
Tecophilaeaceae in nuclear analyses, but its placement
has been equivocal in some plastid analyses (e.g., Chase
et al., 2006), and a sister relationship of these two in
analyses of nuclear DNA is concordant with their similar
morphology (leafy stems, bulbs, and ﬂowers with mostly
inferior ovaries). Tecophilaeaceae are therefore expanded here to include Ixioliriaceae. Morphologically,
Doryanthes Correa (Doryanthaceae; two species) also ﬁts
well in Tecophilaeaceae, despite its large plant size, but
without clear support from nuclear DNA analyses, we
refrain from making this change.
3. In plastid trees (Barrett et al., 2016), Dasypogonaceae
are a strongly supported sister of Arecaceae, but in the
nuclear results (Zuntini et al., 2024), the position of the
former is associated with gene‐tree discordance. As in
APG IV (2016), we include Dasypogonaceae in Arecales,
but we acknowledge that this is a problematic placement that may yet need adjustment.
4. Piperales are another order in which holoparasites, that
is, Hydnoraceae, have been problematic. Hydnoraceae
have been shown in many analyses to be embedded in
a broadly circumscribed Aristolochiaceae (reviewed in
Naumann et al., 2013, and conﬁrmed in Jost et al., 2021),
when using genes from all three genomic compartments, but in Helmstetter et al. (2025), the favored
topology for Hydnoraceae (coalescent analyses) was as
a well‐supported sister to the rest of Piperales.
However, considerable gene‐tree discordance was
noted (35% of gene trees favored the embedded
position in Aristolochiaceae), and some analyses (e.g.,
the concatenated matrix) supported the APG IV (2016)
classiﬁcation. The two genera of Hydnoraceae, Hydnora
Thunb. and Prosopanche de Bary, share diagnostic
characters with Aristolochia L. and Asarum L.: trimerous
ﬂowers with uniseriate perianth, inferior ovaries, and
stamens fused with the style (gynostemium). We
continue to treat Hydnoraceae here as members of
Aristolochiaceae, but note that more research is
required to clarify their relationships. Lactoris Phil. is
embedded in Aristolochiaceae in the analyses of Zuntini
et al. (2024) and Helmstetter et al. (2025), conﬁrming
their placement in APG IV (2016). If Lactoridaceae were
to be recognized (as in Jost et al., 2021), then Asaraceae
would also need to be recognized, breaking with a long
tradition of their placement in Aristolochiaceae. We

5. Relationships of Chloranthaceae have long been problematic, but they are well supported as sister to magnoliids in
several recent nuclear DNA analyses (Guo et al., 2021;
Zuntini et al., 2024), necessitating continued recognition
of Chloranthales. A series of studies revealed incongruence between nuclear and plastid DNA topologies
regarding the placement of magnoliids + Chloranthaceae
relative to other members of the large mesangiosperm
clade. Plastid trees place magnoliids + Chloranthaceae as
sister to monocots plus eudicots, whereas nuclear genes
usually recover monocots as sister to a large clade of
magnoliids, Chloranthales, and eudicots (reviewed in Stull
et al., 2023). Based on complete nuclear genomesequence analyses, Guo et al. (2021) found evidence
that ancient hybridization is the likely source of
discordance over the position of Chloranthales +
magnoliids relative to eudicots and monocots.
6. Although placement of Ceratophyllaceae was considered
problematic in some early analyses with only a few
genes or a few taxa, a series of both plastid and nuclear
gene analyses has provided moderate to strong support
for the family as sister to eudicots (e.g., Soltis et al., 2011;
One Thousand Plant Transcriptomes Initiative, 2019;
Yang et al., 2020; Guo et al., 2021), a placement that
we follow here. However, Zuntini et al. (2024) recovered
Ceratophyllum L. (but with little support) as sister to all
angiosperms, except the ANA grade (Amborellales,
Nymphaeales, and Austrobaileyales), a relationship also
recovered in four nuclear gene analyses using 457 to 922
genes, but not in three others (Xiang et al., 2024). In
analyses of nuclear data using model‐based methods,
Wang et al. (2025) placed Ceratophyllum as sister to the
monocots, but changing the model in this study changed
the placement, leading to little conﬁdence in the
monocots sister to Ceratophyllum result. The diﬃculty
in placing Ceratophyllum may be due to early and rapid
radiation of the mesangiosperms, as noted by Li et al.
(2019). We include Ceratophyllaceae under the informal
category of “ceratophyllids” in the classiﬁcation (below)
rather than including them in the eudicots and note that
the position of Ceratophyllum requires further investigation before it can be classiﬁed conﬁdently.
