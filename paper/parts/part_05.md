8. Peraceae (ﬁve genera, 106 species; POWO, 2025) were
segregated from Euphorbiaceae in APG IV (2016) due to
the interdigitation of Raﬄesiaceae, but in Zuntini et al.
(2024), Peraceae (represented by Clutia L. and Chaetocarpus Thwaites) are again sister to Euphorbiaceae, so
we return the former to synonymy of the latter. As

9. Huaceae were formerly placed in Oxalidales (APG IV), but
this position was not always well supported in plastid‐only
trees. In Li et al. (2021; plastomes only), they were a
member of the larger COM (Celastrales/Oxalidales/
Malpighiales) clade (as in APG IV, 2016), but the interrelationships of these orders were unclear. In Zuntini et al.
(2024), Huaceae were closely related to Celastrales and
Malpighiales, but not Oxalidales (see point 18 below);
however, their speciﬁc placement is not well supported.
Until relationships are better resolved, Huaceae should be
considered a family unplaced to order. Recognition of
Huales Doweld in Li et al. (2021), which was followed by
Zuntini et al. (2024), is not followed here, due to the
uncertain placement of Huaceae.
10. Relationships in Celastrales were studied by Simmons
et al. (2023) using a combined analysis of DNA sequences
and morphology; they recommended recognition of two
families, Parnassiaceae (Parnassia L. and Lepuropetalon
Elliott) and Celastraceae, but this classiﬁcation is not
followed here because Celastraceae s.l. have been a
consistent feature since APG III (2009).
11. Santalales have continued to be problematic. In Zuntini
et al. (2024), taxon sampling was limited, and previous
results with a small number of plastid genes and nuclear
18S rDNA were not reproduced; some of the narrowly
circumscribed families (summarized in Nickrent et al., 2010;
updated in Su et al., 2015; and Nickrent et al., 2019) were
not monophyletic. Olacaceae and Santalaceae sensu APG
IV were known to be polyphyletic, but APG IV refrained
from accepting the numerous small families proposed by
Nickrent at al. (2010) because these were proposed
without knowing their inter‐relationships, which, if known,
might make other options with fewer families viable. The
families of Nickrent et al. (2010) were stated by the authors
to be largely without obvious synapomorphies, and in such
cases, fewer poorly circumscribed families would be
preferable. Cauz‐Santos et al. (2026) realigned the Su
et al. (2015) matrix and found good support (83%
bootstrap) for an Olacaceae clade (including Aptandraceae,
Coulaceae, Octoknemaceae, and Ximeniaceae; all sensu
Nickrent et al., 2010). Anderson et al. (2025), using Hyb‐Seq
nuclear data, found results similar to those in Cauz‐Santos

12. In Caryophyllales, Corbichoniaceae Thulin were proposed
as a new family (Thulin et al., 2016) because of the
morphological distinctiveness of Corbichonia Scop. relative
to Lophiocarpus (Kunth) Miq. (Lophiocarpaceae). Lophiocarpus is wind‐pollinated, and Corbichonia is insectpollinated, syndromes common in larger families such as
Caryophyllaceae and Amaranthaceae, to name a few, so
this seems likely to be the cause of their dissimilarity and
not the basis for separation. We continue to treat
Corbichonia in Lophiocarpaceae despite the lack of
morphological synapomorphies with Lophiocarpus.
13. Frankeniaceae comprise just Frankenia L., which is
distinguished from Tamaricaceae (four genera) by
opposite versus alternate leaves, respectively. They
share small leaves with salt‐excreting glands, basally
clawed petals with a scale‐like appendage or ligule on
the upper side of the claw, secondary chemistry, and
pollen morphology. They have always been considered a
closely related family pair (e.g., Cronquist, 1981) and to
reduce redundancy, we combine them under Frankeniaceae, which has nomenclatural priority.
14. In earlier iterations of APG, we failed to note that
Schäferhoﬀ et al. (2009) showed with two plastid
markers that Agdestis Moç. & Sessé ex DC. is sister to
Sarcobatus Nees, apart from Phytolaccaceae. A recent
study using whole plastomes of Caryophyllales (Yao
et al., 2019) conﬁrmed these relationships, and Zuntini
et al. (2024) found a similar result with nuclear DNA
data. We must therefore either place Sarcobataceae
(including Agdestis) in Phytolaccaceae or accept Agdestidaceae as an additional family. Here, we include
Agdestis and Sarcobatus in Phytolaccaceae, despite the
lack of clear synapomorphies linking Sarcobatus to
Phytolaccaceae, because Agdestis sits comfortably in
Phytolaccaceae on morphological grounds.

16. As noted above, Swanepoel et al. (2020) showed that
Batis P.Brown., the sole genus of Bataceae, was well
supported as sister to Azima Lam. (Salvadoraceae) and
this pair sister to Salvadora, on which basis, we enlarge
Salvadoraceae to include Batis. These taxa also share
many morphological (inﬂorescence) features, making
this a more reasonable change. Sampling in Zuntini et al.
(2024) was not adequate to assess these relationships,
but the position of Batis in Salvadoraceae has been
consistent in all other analyses.
17. The position of the newly described Tiganophytaceae
(Swanepoel et al., 2020) has diﬀered depending on the
genome/loci sequenced. They are either sister to Salvadoraceae (including Bataceae; Swanepoel et al., 2020) or
embedded with Bataceae in a paraphyletic Salvadoraceae
(Zuntini et al., 2024). Not all genera of Salvadoraceae have
been included; most notably, Dobera Raf. is missing, but
unpublished analyses by the PAFTOL project at Royal
Botanic Gardens, Kew, place Dobera as sister to Salvadora
Garcin ex L. (O. Maurin, pers., comm.). Morphologically,
the three families share many ﬂoral characteristics and
could be united, but given what is known thus far,
recognition of Tiganophytaceae is appropriate.
18. In rosids, plastid results (e.g., Li et al., 2021) recovered
the malvids and fabids as sister clades, but mitochondrial
and nuclear results demonstrated a more complicated
evolutionary scenario. Sun et al. (2015) ﬁrst provided
evidence that deep reticulation may have occurred in the
early divergence of rosid clades; members of the COM
families as deﬁned in the plastid analyses (Celastrales,
Oxalidales, Malpighiales) do not form a clade in Zuntini
et al. (2024), and all three fall in the malvids rather than
the fabids (the plastid tree placement). The nuclear trees
of Zeng et al. (2017) further called into question the
circumscriptions of these two large clades. Other, more
recent nuclear DNA trees (e.g., One Thousand Plant
Transcriptomes Initiative, 2019; Yang et al., 2020; Xiang
et al., 2024; Zuntini et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2025) have
indicated that fabids should be limited just to the
nitrogen‐ﬁxing members, Cucurbitales, Rosales, Fagales,
and Fabales. This result was also obtained in the large
mitochondrial analysis of Lin et al. (2025). In the malvids,
there remains a core, well‐supported clade of Malvales,
Brassicales, Sapindales, and Oxalidales, but nuclear and
mitochondrial results also place with them Huerteales,
Picramniales, Huaceae, Malpighiales, and Celastrales
(Zuntini et al., 2024; Lin et al., 2025). These additional
orders plus Huaceae could be included in an enlarged
malvid clade, but placements of Crossosomatales,
Geraniales, Myrtales, and Zygophyllales are less clear,
appearing in some trees as sister to both the
reconﬁgured fabids and expanded malvids. Apart from
the placement of Huaceae, none of these altered
placements aﬀects family or ordinal circumscriptions of
APG IV, and our summary tree (Fig. 1) reﬂects these
altered relationships found in the nuclear‐gene analyses.

19. A similar problem to point 18 above occurs with the
positions of Caryophyllales and Santalales, which, in
plastid results (Li et al., 2021), were included in the
superasterid clade along with Berberidopsidales and the
asterids, but in nuclear and mitochondrial results, it is
unclear where Caryophyllales and Santalales should be
placed. Berberidopsidales are still sister to the large
asterid clade and thus can be included in the superasterid clade, but we consider Caryophyllales and
Santalales unplaced in the larger informally named
superrosids and superasterids. Again, none of this
uncertainty in placements aﬀects the familial or ordinal
circumscriptions of APG IV.
20. In APG IV, Vitales and Saxifragales were included in the
rosids and superrosids, respectively, reﬂecting their
positions in the plastid results (Zhang et al., 2016). In
the mitochondrial trees, they are sister orders placed
outside both the rosids and asterids (Lin et al., 2025).
However, in the nuclear trees (Xiang et al., 2024; Zuntini
et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2025), their positions are reversed
relative to the plastid results, with Vitales and Saxifragales
successively sister to the rosids. We therefore include
both here in the superrosids, as close relatives of the
rosids. As for the orders and families discussed in points
18 and 19, this does not aﬀect circumscriptions in APG IV.
21. APG V continues to recognize Boraginaceae in the
broadest sense, synonymous with the monofamilial
order Boraginales (APG IV, 2016). The split of Boraginaceae into 11 or nine families was argued by Luebert et al.
(2016) and Vasile et al. (2025), respectively, to be the
most conservative and consistent (less disruptive) with
past treatments of the group. In both Vasile et al. (2025)
and Cohen et al. (2025), there were shown to be
problems with both the families as circumscribed by
Luebert et al. (2016) and placement of some relative to
the others (e.g., the position of Namaceae is weakly
supported). For most botanists and other users of APG,
nearly all the segregate families of Luebert et al. (2016)
are unfamiliar. For example, in the treatment of
Boraginaceae for the ﬂora of New Caledonia, Heine
(1976) and Gotty & Pillon (2025) rejected segregate
families, the former stating (translated from the French)
that “The family is understood here in its traditional
sense (A.P. De Candolle, Bentham & J.D. Hooker, Gürke
& Engler); the dismemberments often proposed from R.
Brown ex Dumortier (Cordiaceae, 1829) and by Lindley
(Ehretiaceae, 1830) are not taken into consideration,
given that they are of little theoretical interest and their
acceptance would ruin the incontestable homogeneity
of this group of gamopetalous phanerogams with very
natural systematic and phylogenetic relationships that
are easy to bring together in the original conception of
the Boraginaceae family (Jussieu 1789).” We agree with
these sentiments, despite their age, and hence continue
to follow the traditional treatment of Boraginaceae in
the broad sense outlined in APG IV. We acknowledge
that a subfamilial classiﬁcation for Boraginaceae would
be useful to incorporate what is known about relationships of the proposed separate families.
