3.1 APG‐based subfamily and suborder classiﬁcations
Classiﬁcations are conventions, and APG classiﬁcations are
not exempt from this observation. APG was originally
established to facilitate communication about phylogenetics
of the angiosperms. Words and names are always subject to
changes of meaning, but unnecessary modiﬁcations impede
communication. An alternative to advocating the recognition
of non‐APG orders and families, which complements rather
than conﬂicts with APG, would be to develop infra‐ordinal
and infra‐familial classiﬁcations. These can provide specialists
and interested general users with a framework reﬂecting ongoing discoveries of new clades requiring recognition,
following the models of Colli‐Silva et al. (2025) for Malvales
(especially Malvaceae sensu APG), Larridon et al. (2021) for
Cyperaceae, Buerki et al. (2021) for Sapindaceae, GPWG
(2001, 2012, 2025) for Poaceae, LPWG (2017) and Koenen
et al. (2020) for Fabaceae, and Pillon et al. (2024) for
Oxalidales. In addition, monophyly of most subfamilies in
several of the largest angiosperm families, including
Asteraceae (Mandel et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2021; Zhang
et al., 2024), Orchidaceae (Zhang et al., 2023; Pérez‐Escobar
et al., 2024), Fabaceae (Koenen et al. 2020; Zhao et al., 2021),
and Poaceae (Huang et al., 2022; Zhang et al. 2022; GPWG
III, 2025), has been supported by large‐scale nuclear gene
analyses. Of course, recognition of distinct segregate families
may draw the attention of more general users, but it often
results in less attention for some groups because, for
students and generalist users, the number of families
becomes too large to grasp easily and causes confusion.
Smaller families are typically not covered in systematic
botany and plant identiﬁcation courses because the textbooks that cover these topics (e.g., Spichiger et al., 2004;
Judd et al., 2015; Pandey & Kasana, 2021) cover roughly only a
quarter of the APG families (i.e., never more than 100
families, mostly the larger ones). However, local ﬂora
courses or wild‐ﬂower guides often include some small
families as a local component. As an example of the problem
caused by splitting a family, in most courses on plant
taxonomy, Boraginaceae s.l. (roughly 1800 species) are
covered, but if split in up to 11 families (Luebert et al., 2016),
not more than one of these families (eight with fewer than
ﬁve genera) is likely to be included. Input from nonBoraginaceae specialists in an on‐line survey (Christenhusz
et al., 2015) revealed a preference for a single family for
teaching purposes.
3.2 Condensation of small orders/families
A minor caveat to this principle of stability is when a
monogeneric family or monofamilial order is found to be
sister to another APG family or order and the two can be
combined, especially when families share synapomorphies
(e.g., combining Ixioliriaceae with Tecophilaeaceae and
Frankeniaceae with Tamaricaceae; Backlund & Bremer, 1998).

These actions are taken to increase the phylogenetic content
of the system and reduce redundancy. This is not a new
procedure, and it was also a tradition established in many
past morphological classiﬁcations, for example, Cronquist
(1981), to avoid assigning the rank of family to small groups if
they can be accommodated in a related larger group. A
higher taxon, for example, family or order, for a monospeciﬁc genus or monogeneric family was termed a
“pseudon” by Corner (1981); it is thus a “pseudotaxon” or
supererogatory taxon. In some cases, there is no choice in
this matter (e.g., Amborella and Ceratophyllum), but these
should be avoided. The shifting synapomorphies involved
when combining orders are usually unclear at this point
because many existing APG orders lack shared characteristics, but these are cases on which future studies could be
focused.
3.3 Branching dates as guides for determining taxonomic
ranks
The use of ages has been invoked by some authors as a
criterion for determining an appropriate rank for clades and
has been widely discussed in the taxonomic literature. The
use of “time or temporal banding” to standardize ranks was
supported in some cases by Vences et al. (2013) and for more
general use by Avise & Mitchell (2007) and Kraichak et al.
(2017). Angiosperm families have been demonstrated to have
evolved at many diﬀerent times (Wikström et al., 2001;
Magallón et al. 2015, Zuntini et al., 2024), and any attempt to
equalize the ages of families and orders recognized would
result in massive, chaotic changes to the APG classiﬁcation.
Furthermore, as demonstrated in Magallón et al. (2015),
diﬀerent molecular dating methods have continued to
provide divergent age estimates, and some models have
led to widely diﬀering age estimates for the same family. We
consider attempts to equalize ages of families to be
inappropriate (Lücking, 2019), and this procedure is not
considered further here.
3.4 Conﬂicts in tree topologies
For purposes of classiﬁcation, unambiguous, well‐supported
large‐scale nuclear genomic trees are to be favored over
plastid results if topologies conﬂict. As mentioned above,
earlier versions of APG were primarily plastid‐based by
necessity, and general congruence among results using the
three plant genomes has been noted and considered
corroborative, albeit with some exceptions. Nuclear data
sets analyzing general relationships across angiosperms such
as Zeng et al. (2014) based on a relatively small number of
taxa (61) and genes (59) ﬁrst identiﬁed possible incongruence between nuclear and plastid topologies, as have
other more focused studies, such as the analysis of
mitochondrial genes for rosids by Sun et al. (2015) and all
angiosperms in Lin et al. (2025). The much larger sampling of
taxa and genes aﬀorded by the 1KP project (410 nuclear
orthologs; One Thousand Plant Transcriptomes Initiative, 2019), angiosperm‐wide target sequence capture
analyses of the Plant and Fungal Trees of Life (PAFTOL)
project (Zuntini et al., 2024), and other studies of taxonomically less extensive datasets of nuclear genes (e.g., Stull
et al., 2020; Guo et al., 2021; Alzate et al. 2024; Kates
et al. 2024) have further reinforced the early indications of

3.5 Plastid and nuclear trees
In APG V, we base alterations of APG IV (2016) on wellsupported nuclear results, particularly if also supported by
large analyses of plastome data (Li et al., 2021). The results
on which circumscription of APG V orders and families are
based have been generally consistent across phylogenetic
methods and assumptions, and are parallel in coalescent,
concatenated (combined), and most forms of weighted
analyses. If extensive nuclear gene‐tree incongruence has
been detected, such that the position of a group is
unresolved or only weakly supported, then we indicate
uncertainty of result by considering them as “unplaced” to
order or major clade (e.g., fabids, asterids). Likewise, if a set

3.6 Reproducibility
Reproducibility in a classiﬁcation is a desirable attribute (i.e.,
diﬀerent authors reaching the same ideas of taxon delimitation
based on the same data), but the idiosyncratic nature of plant
classiﬁcation makes this a diﬃcult goal to achieve. The initial
APG classiﬁcation (1998) did not start with a clean slate, and all
iterations of APG have been mixtures of historical concepts
and new ideas about relationships. Many small family pairs, for
