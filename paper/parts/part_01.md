Abstract We present here a revision of the APG classiﬁcation that considers the extensive recent analyses of hundreds
of nuclear and plastid genes for many angiosperm species. Although previous versions of the APG classiﬁcation were
largely based on uniparentally inherited markers (plastid DNA, typically maternally inherited), there has emerged since
APG IV extensive evidence of widespread hybridization and inheritance of ancestral polymorphisms. Despite this
evidence of gene‐tree discordance, most of the APG IV classiﬁcation is supported by both plastid and nuclear analyses,
and only a few revisions are required in this update to make the classiﬁcation parallel phylogenetic results in the
literature. At the ordinal level, few changes in circumscription are required relative to the last APG version, although the
fabids now comprise only the four nitrogen‐ﬁxing orders (Cucurbitales, Fabales, Fagales, and Rosales) and the malvids
now include the former COM clade (Celastrales, Malpighiales, and Oxalidales), which, in nuclear DNA analyses, is no
longer monophyletic. Oncothecales (with only Oncothecaceae) and Cardiopteridales (with Cardiopteridaceae and
Stemonuraceae) are newly recognized, and Icacinales are restricted to Icacinaceae. Restriction of Aquifoliales and
Bruniales to just Aquifoliaceae/Helwingiaceae and Bruniaceae, respectively, is also proposed. Huaceae and Columelliaceae
are unplaced to order among the malvids and campanulids, respectively. At the family level, expanded circumscriptions
include Tecophilaeaceae (including Ixioliriaceae, Asparagales), Frankeniaceae (including Tamaricaceae, Caryophyllales),

1 Introduction
The goal of modern biological classiﬁcation is to translate
phylogenetic knowledge into an explicit system for communicating about biological taxa, and the challenges involved in
conveying incomplete and imperfect knowledge through a
universal system are faced by systematists working across the
tree of life. Moreover, competing classiﬁcations for a group of
organisms can impede communication and stiﬂe use of any of
these. Thus, the issues of which taxa to recognize and name—
and the criteria governing these decisions—are common to
classiﬁcation of all organismal groups, and successful strategies
and methods developed for one group may be applicable to
other groups. Here, we present an updated version of the
Angiosperm Phylogeny Group (APG) classiﬁcation, a system ﬁrst
published almost three decades ago with explicit criteria and a
community‐based approach to classiﬁcation. The principle of
generating a classiﬁcation through developing consensus of
many specialists was a new concept at that time in the ﬁeld
of classiﬁcation, which by and large had single authors,
especially in the last 100 years. APG (1998) was the ﬁrst such
classiﬁcation to involve numerous authors and broad community input and has served as an exemplar for many other
ﬁelds (e.g., Pteridophyte Phylogeny Group, 2016); now, APG V
provides a framework for incorporation of taxonomically broad
studies of nuclear genes as the basis for a revised classiﬁcation
of angiosperms, a model that other groups may wish to follow.
Previous classiﬁcations of ﬂowering plants from the
Angiosperm Phylogeny Group (APG I–IV; 1998, 2003, 2009,
2016) relied almost exclusively on molecular phylogenetic studies
of DNA regions from the plastid genome, ﬁrst rbcL and then atpB
(Chase et al., 1993; Savolainen et al., 2000a, 2000b). Plastid
markers were chosen because there are many plastids per cell
with a single genome per plastid, and they have a rate of
molecular evolution appropriate for phylogenetic inference of
higher‐order relationships (Palmer, 1987; Ritland & Clegg, 1987).
The availability of free, universal rbcL PCR and sequencing
primers also made it inexpensive and easy to amplify and
sequence this gene (Zurawski & Clegg, 1987). In contrast, most
nuclear genes were diﬃcult to sequence at that time, resulting in
a focus on the highly repeated nuclear ribosomal RNA genes,
which were included in some early studies (e.g., Hamby &
Zimmer, 1992; Soltis et al., 1997). However, ribosomal trees
showed lower resolution and support than the plastid trees,
given the slower rate of evolution of ribosomal DNA (as opposed
to the more rapidly evolving spacers such as nuclear ribosomal
ITS). Because these early nuclear phylogenetic trees were largely
congruent with inferences based on plastid genes (Nickrent &
Soltis, 1995; Kuzoﬀ et al., 1998), they were often combined with

plastid gene sequences (e.g., Soltis et al., 2000). Ultimately, a
17‐gene data set (Soltis et al., 2011) in concert with other
published analyses, many involving numerous plastid (Yang
et al., 2014; Sun et al., 2016) and mitochondrial (Zhu et al., 2007)
genes, formed the backbone tree compiled for APG IV (2016).
Although inherited as a single unit with phylogenetically
negligible levels of recombination (Doyle, 2022; but see Li
et al., 2025, for a rare exception), plastid genomes (and to a
lesser extent mitochondrial genomes) are nevertheless a rich
source of phylogenetic information. However, we have long
dealt with the reality that organellar genomes eﬀectively mask
evidence of hybridization and other potentially confounding
processes (e.g., incomplete lineage sorting, ILS). Compounding
this problem was that, historically, the main sources of nuclear
gene trees were those of nuclear ribosomal RNA/DNA, which are
generally subject to concerted evolution (Hills & Dixon, 1991) and
thus also concealed evidence of reticulation.
Although the general congruence of plastid, mitochondrial,
and nuclear topologies has long been noted, it has been
recognized that ultimately, the APG classiﬁcation would need
to encompass results from an extensive sampling of nuclear
genes (APG, 2016). During the past decade, the rapid
accumulation of large data sets obtained from transcriptomes
(e.g., Zeng et al., 2014, 2017; One Thousand Plant Transcriptomes Initiative, 2019; Yang et al., 2020; Zhang et al., 2020;
Xiang et al., 2024), target sequence capture (e.g., Baker
et al., 2022; Zuntini et al., 2024; Kates et al., 2024), and
complete or near‐complete genome assemblies (e.g., Guo
et al., 2021; Kress et al., 2022; Lewin et al., 2022; Darwin Tree of
Life Project Consortium, 2022) has provided a broad sampling
of nuclear genes across angiosperms. These massive and
rapidly growing nuclear data sets have brought us to a pivotal
moment in the history of APG: a shift toward reliance on the
nuclear genome to inform classiﬁcation. In general, congruence of analyses of large numbers of nuclear and
organellar genes provides greater conﬁdence in proposing a
revised classiﬁcation, and if incongruence is detected,
examination of nuclear genes is essential for understanding
the evolutionary processes behind this discordance.
As a general summary of the novelties in this update of
APG, we have observed well‐supported relationships in
multiple nuclear gene trees (e.g., Zuntini et al., 2024) that
mostly agree with previous plastid trees (e.g., Li et al., 2021;
reviewed in Zhang & Ma, 2024), despite the presence of
incongruence and evidence of deep reticulation at various
points (Stull et al., 2020, 2023; Zuntini et al., 2024). We wish
